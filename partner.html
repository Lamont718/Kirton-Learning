Before anything else — two things to check on your side

1. Whether you're allowed to do this at all

If you're employed by a school, a district, or an agency, having an ownership stake in a company that sells to families — possibly families you serve — is a different order of problem than referring someone. Most public employers require written approval for outside business activity, and some prohibit a financial interest outright. If any part of your work is billed to Medicaid, the rules are tighter still.

Please check your own employer's conflict-of-interest and outside-employment policy before we go further. I'd rather lose the partnership than cost you your job or your license, and I'd rather you find this out now than after we've signed something.

2. That we'd both want this in writing

A handshake is how friendships end. If we do this, it should be a written operating agreement drawn up by a lawyer, covering who owns what, who decides what, what happens to the work if one of us leaves, and how either of us gets out without a fight. I'll pay for that to be done properly.

My nonprofit stays out of it entirely. A 501(c)(3) can't hand anybody an ownership stake, and mixing the two would put fifteen years of that organization at risk.

What we'd have to decide together

I haven't filled these in, on purpose. If I hand you a finished deal, it's my company and you're an employee with a nicer title. These are the real questions and I'd rather answer them across a table.

  1. What each of us actually does every week.

    Who reads the IEP, who builds the plan, who talks to the parent, who fixes it when a family is unhappy. Roles before percentages — the split should follow the work, not the other way round.

  2. How much time you can genuinely give.

    Honestly, including the weeks you can't. This decides whether we're aiming at five families or fifty.

  3. Ownership, and in what.

    A share of Our Rose LLC gives you a piece of everything that entity does, which is probably not what either of us means. A new entity for this specific business is likely cleaner. That's a conversation with a lawyer, not a number I should pick alone.

  4. Who owns the method if this ends.

    I built the platform and the approach; you'd be shaping the clinical side. Both of us should know now who walks away with what.

  5. Money in, and money out.

    What we each put in, whether either of us is paid before the business is profitable, and what we do in the months it makes nothing. It will make nothing for a while.

  6. What "it worked" means.

    Ten paying families? A measurement design you'd stand behind? A number we agree on now, so we can tell later whether to keep going.

What I'm asking for

Not a yes. A yes right now would be worth nothing, because you haven't seen enough to give one.

Read the example year. Then one conversation, an hour, where you tell me what's wrong with it — particularly the measurement side, where I already know I'm weak. If after that you want to talk about building it together, we'll go through the six questions above properly, and get a lawyer to write down whatever we land on.

And if the honest answer is that you'd rather send me a family or two and keep your distance from the business, say that plainly. It's a good answer and it costs us nothing.
