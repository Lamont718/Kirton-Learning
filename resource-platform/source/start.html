You are here

Everything in this system runs on your child's age. Start there.

Which committee handles your child, what they're entitled to, and who to call all change at three and again at five. Pick the one that fits and you'll get the right next step instead of everybody else's.

Birth to age 3

Early Intervention, run by the Health Department — not the schools

This is the one part of the system that isn't run by the Department of Education. It's free, and services can happen at home or wherever your child already spends the day.

  1. Make the referral. You can refer your own child. You don't need a doctor's permission and you don't need a diagnosis first.
  2. An evaluation gets scheduled. It's free to you.
  3. If your child is eligible, you and the team write an IFSP — an Individualized Family Service Plan. Note the word family. At this age the plan supports you too, not only the child.
  4. Around age 2½, start asking about the handoff. Early Intervention stops at 3. Preschool special education is a different system with a different committee, and the switch isn't automatic if nobody starts it.

Timing: children already in Early Intervention should be referred for a preschool evaluation about four months before they become eligible for preschool services. Don't wait for someone to bring it up.

Ages 3 to 5

CPSE — the Committee on Preschool Special Education

CPSE covers preschool-age children. Your CPSE is assigned by where your child lives. There are eleven CSE/CPSE offices across the city, and each one covers three or four districts.

  1. Refer in writing. Send a letter to the CPSE that serves your district saying who your child is and why you're referring. Keep a copy of everything you send, always.
  2. Consent to the evaluation. Once you sign consent for an initial evaluation, the district has 60 calendar days to complete it. That clock is in the regulations. Write down the date you signed.
  3. Go to the meeting. You are a member of the committee, not a guest at it. Bring every report you have.
  4. If eligible, an IEP is written and services begin. They can be delivered in a preschool program, a Head Start, a child care setting you chose, or at home.

Start dates run on a calendar: a child turning 3 between January 1 and June 30 can begin preschool services January 2; a child turning 3 between July 1 and December 31 can begin July 1.

The year before kindergarten

Turning 5 — where families fall through the floor

This is the handoff from preschool special education to school-age special education, and it is the single most common place we see families lose services. New committee, new criteria, new paperwork, on a deadline nobody explains.

  1. Winter of the year before kindergarten, it starts. If your child already gets preschool services, CPSE refers your child to CSE automatically. If your child does not get services and you're worried, you make the referral yourself.
  2. Watch for the blue letter. The Notice of Referral goes out between December and August, and families are typically contacted by the IEP team between January and March. If nothing has arrived by February 1, call the CSE for your home district. Don't wait politely.
  3. Consent to reevaluation. Preschool eligibility and school-age eligibility use different criteria — a child who qualified at 3 does not automatically qualify at 5, and services that existed in preschool don't automatically carry over.
  4. The transition meeting happens before your child's fifth birthday, usually somewhere between January and June.
  5. Bring paper. Every evaluation, every progress report, every therapy note. The new committee doesn't know your child.

The hard deadline: your child's first school-age IEP must be finalized by the fifth birthday. Every delay upstream — a late consent, a slow evaluation — eats into that. This is why February matters.

Ages 5 to 21

CSE — the Committee on Special Education

School-age special education runs to age 21 or high school completion. For most school-age students you're assigned to the CSE where your child goes to school — unless your child is homeschooled, attends school outside the city, or isn't attending school, in which case it's based on where you live.

  1. Suspect a problem? Refer in writing. Put the date on it. A verbal request to a teacher is not a referral.
  2. Consent starts the 60-day clock for the evaluation to be completed.
  3. The IEP meeting sets goals, services, and placement. You're a member of that team.
  4. The IEP gets reviewed at least once a year, and your child is reevaluated at least every three years unless you and the district agree otherwise.
  5. Disagree? You have real options — an independent evaluation, mediation, an impartial hearing. Talk to one of the free organizations below before you sign anything you're unsure about.

If your child attends a private, parochial, or charter school in NYC, or is homeschooled, your case sits with a CSE/CPSE office rather than a school-based team.

Placement

"Which school should I look at for my autistic child?"

This is the question we hear most, and the honest answer starts before the school list. Placement in New York is a continuum, and the law says you start at the least restrictive end and only move along it when there's a documented reason to.

Least restrictive

General education

A regular class, with related services and supports written into the IEP. Most children with disabilities attend their ordinary neighborhood school.

More support

ICT — integrated co-teaching

One general education teacher and one special education teacher in the same room, serving students with and without IEPs together.

More support

Special class in a community school

A smaller self-contained class inside a regular school building. Programs like ASD Nest and ASD Horizon sit around here.

Most restrictive

District 75

The citywide district for students with the most significant disabilities. Small ratios, built-in therapy, roughly 23,000 students across dozens of schools and hundreds of sites.

An autism classification does not by itself mean District 75. Most students with disabilities are served in ordinary NYC public schools. D75 is one end of a continuum, not the default destination for a diagnosis — and the IEP team, which includes you, decides where on that continuum your child belongs.

What people say: "He's autistic, so he'll go to District 75."

What's actually true: the team recommends a setting based on your child's needs, and the least restrictive appropriate option comes first. Some District 75 students spend part of their day in general education classes with their peers. Some autistic children never go near D75. Both are normal.

What people say: "There's a list of autism schools I should apply to."

What's actually true: it doesn't work like admissions. Placement follows the IEP. If a District 75 program is recommended, the school team or the CSE refers your child, and placement is generally within your borough as close to home as possible. There are specialized programs worth asking about by name — ASD Nest, ASD Horizon, AIMS — but you ask the IEP team about them, you don't apply the way you would to a school.

Who's who

"I don't know which professional I'm supposed to call."

Here's what each of these people actually does. We're describing the roles — which one your child needs is a question for your pediatrician and your child's evaluation team, not for a website.

SLP

Speech-language pathologist

Communication — speech sounds, understanding and using language, and for some children, communication devices and systems.

OT

Occupational therapist

The work of daily life at your child's age: fine motor skills, handwriting, dressing, feeding, sensory processing, self-care.

PT

Physical therapist

Gross motor — walking, balance, coordination, strength, moving safely through a school building.

LBA / BCBA

Behavior analyst

Applies behavior analysis to assess and address behavior. In New York this is a licensed health profession with its own credential.

Neuropsych

Neuropsychologist

In-depth testing of thinking, learning, attention, and memory. Often the evaluation that produces a diagnosis or explains a puzzling learning profile.

Dev Peds

Developmental pediatrician

A physician specializing in development. Frequently the doctor who makes or confirms an autism diagnosis.

SEIT

Special education itinerant teacher

A preschool special education teacher who comes to your child's classroom or home rather than your child going to a special class.

Advocate / attorney

Special education advocate

Helps you prepare for and navigate IEP meetings and disputes with the district. Several organizations below do this at no charge.

The decoder

The words they use in the meeting.

Nobody explains these and everybody assumes you know them. You're not behind — the vocabulary is genuinely opaque.

FAPE

Free Appropriate Public Education. The core promise of federal special education law: your child is entitled to an education suited to their needs, at no cost to you. When people talk about a district "denying FAPE," this is the standard they mean.

LRE — and why it matters more than any other four letters

Least Restrictive Environment. Your child should be educated with non-disabled peers to the greatest extent appropriate, and moved to a more separate setting only when the nature of the disability makes that necessary. This is the legal reason a general education classroom is the starting point of the conversation, not a special class.

IFSP vs IEP

An IFSP — Individualized Family Service Plan — is what Early Intervention writes for children under 3, and it supports the family, not only the child. An IEP — Individualized Education Program — is what CPSE and CSE write from age 3 up, and it's focused on the child's education.

Related services

The supports listed on an IEP alongside instruction — speech, OT, PT, counseling, and others. They're mandated once they're on the IEP, which means the district is obligated to deliver them, not merely to intend to.

RSA — and what to do when a service isn't happening

Related Services Authorization. When the district can't staff a service your child's IEP mandates, it can issue an RSA that lets you find an independent provider and have the DOE pay for it. If your child's IEP says speech twice a week and no speech has happened since September, this is the mechanism to ask your school's IEP team or CSE office about.

We're telling you the mechanism exists. For advice about your child's specific situation, one of the free organizations below is the right call.

ICT, SETSS, and the class ratios

ICT is integrated co-teaching — two teachers, one room, mixed group. SETSS is special education teacher support services, delivered to a student in or out of the classroom. The numbers you'll hear — 12:1:1, 8:1:1, 6:1:1, 12:1:4 — are students to teacher to paraprofessional. Smaller first number, more adult attention.

Prior Written Notice (PWN)

The formal written notice the district must send when it proposes or refuses to change your child's identification, evaluation, placement, or services. When something changes, you should get this in writing. Keep every one.

The 60-day clock

After you sign consent for an initial evaluation, the district has 60 calendar days to complete it. Write the consent date on your own calendar. It is the single most useful date you will hold.

What we do and don't do here

Everything on this page explains how the system works — the law, the committees, the deadlines, the vocabulary, who does what job. That's public information, and it should have been handed to you already.

What we don't do is tell you what your particular child needs. We're not therapists, evaluators, or advocates, and a website that answered that question about a child it has never met would be doing you harm dressed up as help. For that, call the organizations above, or your child's evaluation team.
