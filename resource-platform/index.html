The NYC Special Education Handbook

A free, plain-English handbook for parents raising a child with a disability in New York City — and two free tools to use alongside it.

  • It explains how the school system, the money, and your rights actually work — the committees, the deadlines, the vocabulary, and what you can ask for.
  • It does not tell you what your child needs. That belongs to your child's evaluation team. We point you to free people for the rest.
  • It costs nothing. No account, no sign-up, no email, no tracking. Nothing is sold on this page.
  • It was written by Kirton Learning, a Brooklyn company that tutors children with IEPs, because families kept asking us these questions.

Nobody hands you a map when this starts. So here's the map.

Quick answers

The questions parents ask us first.

Short answers, in the order they usually come up. Each one links to the fuller explanation if you want it.

Just starting out
My child was just diagnosed. What's the first thing I should do?

Ask the school district for an evaluation, in writing, today.

That one letter starts a legal clock: once you sign consent, the district has 60 calendar days to complete the evaluation. Everything else — services, an IEP, funding — follows from it. Write the date you sent it on your own copy.

More in School and the IEP →
Do I need a diagnosis before I can get any services?

No. You can ask for an evaluation without one.

Waiting for a diagnosis first is one of the most common ways families lose months. School eligibility is a separate decision from a medical diagnosis, and you can start the school process now.

More in School and the IEP →
Does the evaluation cost me anything?

No. An evaluation by the school district is free to you.

The same is true of Early Intervention for children under 3. If someone is asking you to pay for an evaluation, that is a private evaluation — a different thing, and sometimes useful, but not the one the district owes you.

More in Paying for it →
How do I actually ask? Do I call someone?

Put it in writing. A verbal request to a teacher is not a referral.

Write to the CSE or CPSE office that serves your district — a short letter saying who your child is and why you are asking. Keep a dated copy of everything you send. If you hand it in at the school, ask them to date-stamp your copy.

More in School and the IEP →
School and placement
Can my autistic child go to a regular school?

Yes — and under federal law that's the starting point, not the exception.

Least Restrictive Environment means the question isn't whether your child has earned a general education classroom. It's whether anyone can justify moving her out of one. Most children with disabilities in this city attend their ordinary neighborhood school.

More in Placement →
Will my child be sent to District 75?

Not automatically. An autism classification by itself does not mean District 75.

D75 is one end of a continuum that starts with general education. The IEP team — which includes you — decides where on that continuum your child belongs, and the least restrictive appropriate option comes first.

More in Placement →
Do I have to sign the IEP at the meeting?

You can ask for time to read it before you agree to anything.

You are a member of that committee, not a guest at it. Asking for a copy to review is normal and reasonable. If you are unsure about something in it, one of the free organizations at the bottom of this page will read it with you at no cost.

More in Get help →
Can I bring someone with me to the meeting?

Yes. You can bring anyone you want — a friend, a relative, an advocate.

You do not need permission and you do not need to explain who they are. Many parents find that having a second person taking notes changes how the meeting goes.

What happens when my child turns 5?

Preschool services hand off to school-age services, and it is the most common place families lose ground.

New committee, different eligibility criteria, new paperwork. Watch for the notice between December and August — and if nothing has arrived by February 1, call the CSE for your home district yourself.

Money
Do I have to pay for any of this?

Not for anything written on the IEP. That is the district's obligation, not a favor.

Evaluations, the services listed on the IEP, transportation when the IEP requires it, an interpreter — all free to you. Families routinely pay out of pocket for things they are already owed.

More in Paying for it →
Will my insurance cover therapy?

Many New York plans must — but it depends on one thing most parents have never been asked.

The state mandate applies to fully insured plans, not to self-funded employer plans. The card in your wallet looks the same either way. Call HR and ask: "Is our health plan fully insured or self-funded?"

Do I earn too much to qualify for state help?

Probably not. For a child living at home seeking HCBS Waiver enrollment, parental income is not counted.

Families rule themselves out of years of support based on an assumption nobody ever corrected. If you have never applied because you assumed your salary disqualified your child, that assumption is worth checking with the OPWDD Front Door.

Ask about an RSA before you start paying for it yourself.

When the district can't staff a service your child's IEP mandates, it can issue a Related Services Authorization — a voucher letting you find an independent provider at DOE expense. Ask your school's IEP team or your CSE office.

Yes. An interpreter at meetings and a translated IEP are both rights, on request.

You should never be asked to bring your own interpreter, and your child should never be used as your interpreter. Request an interpreter in writing at least 72 hours before a meeting — there's a free letter tool on this page that writes it for you in nine languages.

If you read nothing else

Overwhelmed and don't know where to start?

Do one thing this week: write to your CSE or CPSE office asking for an evaluation, and keep a dated copy. That single letter starts the clock that everything else depends on.

01

School and the IEP

Everything in this system runs on your child's age. Which committee handles them, what they're entitled to, and who to call all change at three and again at five. Pick the age that fits.

Birth to age 3

Early Intervention, run by the Health Department — not the schools

The one part of the system that isn't run by the Department of Education. Free, and services can happen at home or wherever your child already spends the day.

  1. Make the referral. You can refer your own child. No doctor's permission, no diagnosis needed first.
  2. An evaluation gets scheduled. Free to you.
  3. If eligible, you and the team write an IFSP — an Individualized Family Service Plan. Note the word family. At this age the plan supports you too.
  4. Around age 2½, ask about the handoff. EI stops at 3. Preschool special education is a different system with a different committee, and the switch isn't automatic if nobody starts it.

Children already in Early Intervention should be referred for a preschool evaluation about four months before becoming eligible for preschool services. Don't wait for someone to bring it up.

Ages 3 to 5

CPSE — the Committee on Preschool Special Education

Your CPSE is assigned by where your child lives. There are eleven CSE/CPSE offices across the city, each covering three or four districts.

  1. Refer in writing. A letter to the CPSE serving your district, saying who your child is and why you're referring. Keep a copy of everything.
  2. Consent to the evaluation. Once you sign, the district has 60 calendar days to complete it. Write down the date you signed.
  3. Go to the meeting. You are a member of the committee, not a guest at it. Bring every report you have.
  4. If eligible, an IEP is written and services begin — in a preschool program, a Head Start, a child care setting you chose, or at home.

Start dates run on a calendar: turning 3 between January 1 and June 30 means services can begin January 2; between July 1 and December 31 means July 1.

The year before kindergarten

Turning 5 — where families fall through the floor

The handoff from preschool to school-age special education, and the single most common place we see families lose services. New committee, new criteria, new paperwork, on a deadline nobody explains.

  1. Winter of the year before kindergarten, it starts. If your child already gets preschool services, CPSE refers to CSE automatically. If not, you make the referral yourself.
  2. Watch for the blue letter. The Notice of Referral goes out between December and August; families are typically contacted January to March. If nothing has arrived by February 1, call the CSE for your home district.
  3. Consent to reevaluation. Preschool and school-age eligibility use different criteria — a child who qualified at 3 does not automatically qualify at 5.
  4. The transition meeting happens before the fifth birthday, usually January to June.
  5. Bring paper. Every evaluation, progress report, therapy note. The new committee doesn't know your child.

The hard deadline: the first school-age IEP must be finalized by the fifth birthday. Every delay upstream eats into that. This is why February matters.

Ages 5 to 21

CSE — the Committee on Special Education

School-age special education runs to 21 or high school completion. You're generally assigned to the CSE where your child goes to school — unless they're homeschooled, attend school outside the city, or aren't attending, in which case it's where you live.

  1. Suspect a problem? Refer in writing. Date it. A verbal request to a teacher is not a referral.
  2. Consent starts the 60-day clock for the evaluation.
  3. The IEP meeting sets goals, services, and placement. You're a member of that team.
  4. Reviewed at least yearly, reevaluated at least every three years unless you and the district agree otherwise.
  5. Disagree? You have real options — an independent evaluation, mediation, an impartial hearing. Talk to a free organization before signing anything you're unsure about.

If your child attends a private, parochial, or charter school in NYC, or is homeschooled, your case sits with a CSE/CPSE office rather than a school-based team.

02

"Which school should I look at?"

The most common question we hear, and the honest answer starts before the school list. Placement in New York is a continuum, and the law says you start at the least restrictive end and only move along it when there's a documented reason.

Least restrictive

General education

A regular class with related services and supports written into the IEP. Most children with disabilities attend their ordinary neighborhood school.

More support

ICT — integrated co-teaching

One general education teacher and one special education teacher in the same room, serving students with and without IEPs.

More support

Special class in a community school

A smaller self-contained class inside a regular school building. Programs like ASD Nest and ASD Horizon sit around here.

Most restrictive

District 75

The citywide district for students with the most significant disabilities. Small ratios, built-in therapy, roughly 23,000 students across dozens of schools.

An autism classification does not by itself mean District 75. Most students with disabilities are served in ordinary NYC public schools. D75 is one end of a continuum, not the default destination for a diagnosis — and the IEP team, which includes you, decides where on that continuum your child belongs.

What people say: "He's autistic, so he'll go to District 75."

What's actually true: the team recommends a setting based on your child's needs, and the least restrictive appropriate option comes first. Some District 75 students spend part of their day in general education classes with their peers. Some autistic children never go near D75. Both are normal.

What people say: "There's a list of autism schools I should apply to."

What's actually true: it doesn't work like admissions. Placement follows the IEP. If a District 75 program is recommended, the school team or the CSE refers your child, generally within your borough as close to home as possible. There are specialized programs worth asking about by name — ASD Nest, ASD Horizon, AIMS — but you ask the IEP team, you don't apply.

03

"I don't know which professional to call"

Here's what each of these people actually does. We're describing the roles — which one your child needs is a question for your pediatrician and your child's evaluation team, not for a website.

SLP

Speech-language pathologist

Communication — speech sounds, understanding and using language, and for some children, communication devices and systems.

OT

Occupational therapist

The work of daily life at your child's age: fine motor skills, handwriting, dressing, feeding, sensory processing, self-care.

PT

Physical therapist

Gross motor — walking, balance, coordination, strength, moving safely through a school building.

LBA / BCBA

Behavior analyst

Applies behavior analysis to assess and address behavior. In New York this is a licensed health profession with its own credential.

Neuropsych

Neuropsychologist

In-depth testing of thinking, learning, attention, and memory. Often the evaluation that produces a diagnosis or explains a puzzling learning profile.

Dev Peds

Developmental pediatrician

A physician specializing in development. Frequently the doctor who makes or confirms an autism diagnosis.

SEIT

Special education itinerant teacher

A preschool special education teacher who comes to your child's classroom or home rather than your child going to a special class.

Advocate

Special education advocate

Helps you prepare for and navigate IEP meetings and disputes with the district. Several organizations below do this at no charge.

Before you hire anyone in New York, you can check their license yourself through the NYSED Office of the Professions. It takes a minute and it's worth doing.

04

Paying for it

Families spend thousands on services their district already owes them, and rule themselves out of state funding they'd qualify for. There are three different money systems, and they don't talk to each other. Sort your question first.

System one

The school district

Anything about your child's education. An obligation, not a benefit — provided at no cost to you.

  • Evaluations
  • Everything on the IEP
  • Speech, OT, PT at school
  • Placement
  • Transportation when the IEP requires it
System two

Health insurance

Medical and therapeutic treatment outside school. New York law requires many plans to cover autism treatment.

  • Diagnosis and evaluation
  • ABA, speech, OT, PT in clinic or at home
  • Doctor visits
  • Some equipment and devices
System three

OPWDD and the waiver

Long-term supports funded through the state. The one families most often don't know exists.

  • Respite
  • Community habilitation
  • Assistive technology
  • Home modifications
  • Support into adulthood
Free to you

Start with what already costs nothing

  • The evaluation. Ask in writing. 60 calendar days from the date you sign consent.
  • Early Intervention for children under 3. Free. Refer your own child.
  • Everything written on the IEP. If speech twice a week is on there, it's owed. Not offered — owed.
  • An interpreter and a translated IEP, in your language, on request.
  • An RSA when the district can't staff a mandated service — a voucher to find your own provider at DOE expense.
  • Free advocacy. Advocates for Children and Disability Rights New York cost nothing.

Does my insurance have to cover autism treatment?

Short answer

Many New York plans must — but whether yours does depends on one thing most parents have never been asked.

New York's autism insurance law requires state-regulated health plans to cover the screening, diagnosis, and treatment of autism spectrum disorder. Treatment explicitly includes applied behavior analysis, therapeutic care from licensed speech, occupational, and physical therapists, and assistive communication devices.

Here's the catch

The mandate applies to fully insured, New York State–regulated plans. It generally does not apply to self-funded employer plans governed by federal ERISA law.

The card in your wallet looks identical either way. Large employers are the most likely to self-fund, so a parent with a good job at a big company can have weaker autism coverage than a neighbor on a marketplace plan.

Call HR and ask: "Is our health plan fully insured or self-funded?"

05

OPWDD — the door most families never find

The Office for People With Developmental Disabilities funds long-term supports across a lifetime. Autism can qualify. Getting in has a specific order, and doing it out of order costs months.

  1. Gather your documents, including a psychological evaluation — see the note below about what it must contain.
  2. Contact the Front Door at your regional office. The mandatory entry point for all OPWDD services.
  3. Attend a Front Door information session.
  4. Eligibility determination. You'll receive a letter.
  5. Assessment to identify strengths, needs, and interests.
  6. Choose a Care Coordination Organization. They become your ongoing point of contact.
  7. A Life Plan is written and services begin.
The thing families get wrong

"We make too much money to qualify." Usually, that's not how this works.

For a child living at home seeking enrollment in the HCBS Waiver, parental income and resources are not counted. The waiver looks at the child, not the household.

Families rule themselves out of years of support based on an assumption nobody ever corrected.

Careful with this one: the rule is specific to children living at home seeking HCBS Waiver enrollment. Other OPWDD services have different cost-of-care rules. Confirm your own situation with the Front Door — don't take a website's word for it, including this one.

Ask before the evaluation, not after

Make sure the psychological evaluation includes adaptive behavior testing.

Eligibility generally rests on documentation of substantial functional limitations, and an evaluation without adaptive behavior testing often can't establish that. If it's missing, the process stalls while you go back and get it.

Tell the evaluator up front the report may be used for an OPWDD eligibility determination. Costs nothing to ask; can save months.

Source: NYS Office for People With Developmental Disabilities. opwdd.ny.gov/get-startednot yet verified

07

What to say in the room

Knowing you have a right and knowing how to say it out loud are two different things. These are sentences you can use exactly as written. Say them plainly — you are not being difficult, you are being a member of the committee.

When you need time

They hand you a document and wait for you to sign.

"I'd like to take this home and read it before I agree to anything. When do you need it back?"

Asking for time is normal. The second sentence keeps it collaborative rather than a refusal.

The meeting is moving faster than you can follow.

"Can we slow down? I want to make sure I understand this part before we move on."

Nobody will offer you this. You have to ask, and you are allowed to ask more than once.

Someone uses a term you don't know.

"What does that mean, in plain words?"

Not knowing the acronym is not a failure on your part. Everyone in that room learned it at work.

When they propose a more separate setting

A special class or District 75 is recommended.

"What supports would make general education work for her? I'd like those considered first."

This is the Least Restrictive Environment question, asked out loud. It puts the burden where the law puts it.

You want the reasoning on the record.

"Can you help me understand what was tried in the general education setting, and what happened?"

A more restrictive placement is supposed to follow a documented reason. This asks for it without accusing anyone.

When something is promised

Someone says they'll take care of it.

"That sounds good — can we write that into the IEP?"

The single most useful sentence on this page. A promise in the room is not enforceable. A line in the IEP is.

You want to know what happens if it doesn't get delivered.

"If this service doesn't start on time, who should I contact, and by when?"

Asking in advance is far easier than chasing later, and it tells the room you'll be following up.

When you need help or a change

English isn't your preferred language.

"I need an interpreter in [your language] for this meeting, and a translated copy of the IEP."

Both are rights, on request. Put it in writing at least 72 hours ahead — there's a free letter tool on this page.

A service on the IEP isn't happening.

"The IEP mandates speech twice a week and it hasn't started. Can we discuss an RSA?"

Naming the mechanism shows you know it exists, which often changes how the conversation goes.

You disagree and want it noted.

"I don't agree with that recommendation, and I'd like my disagreement noted in the minutes."

You can disagree without ending the meeting. Getting it on record preserves your options later.

Two things that make all of these work better

Bring a second person. A friend, a relative, anyone. You don't need permission and you don't have to explain who they are. Having someone taking notes changes how a meeting runs.

Write down what was agreed, in the room. Then send a short email afterward: "Thank you for meeting. My understanding is that we agreed to X and Y." That email is now a dated record, and it costs you five minutes.

08

The decoder

The words they use in the meeting. Nobody explains these and everybody assumes you know them. You're not behind — the vocabulary is genuinely opaque.

FAPE

Free Appropriate Public Education. The core promise of federal special education law: your child is entitled to an education suited to their needs, at no cost to you.

LRE — and why it matters more than any other four letters

Least Restrictive Environment. Your child should be educated with non-disabled peers to the greatest extent appropriate, and moved to a more separate setting only when the disability makes that necessary. This is the legal reason a general education classroom is the starting point of the conversation, not a special class.

IFSP vs IEP

An IFSP is what Early Intervention writes for children under 3, and it supports the family, not only the child. An IEP is what CPSE and CSE write from age 3 up, focused on the child's education.

RSA — what to do when a service isn't happening

Related Services Authorization. When the district can't staff a service your child's IEP mandates, it can issue an RSA letting you find an independent provider at DOE expense. If the IEP says speech twice a week and none has happened since September, this is the mechanism to ask your IEP team or CSE office about.

ICT, SETSS, and the class ratios

ICT is integrated co-teaching — two teachers, one room, mixed group. SETSS is special education teacher support services, delivered in or out of the classroom. The numbers — 12:1:1, 8:1:1, 6:1:1, 12:1:4 — are students to teacher to paraprofessional. Smaller first number, more adult attention.

Prior Written Notice (PWN)

The formal written notice the district must send when it proposes or refuses to change your child's identification, evaluation, placement, or services. Keep every one.

The 60-day clock

After you sign consent for an initial evaluation, the district has 60 calendar days to complete it. Write the consent date on your own calendar. It is the single most useful date you will hold.
