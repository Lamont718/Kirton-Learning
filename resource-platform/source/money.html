Track 03 · Paying for it

Most of what your child needs, somebody else pays for.

Families spend thousands on services their school district already owes them, and rule themselves out of state funding they'd qualify for. Here's who pays for what, in plain terms.

Free. No account. Nothing to upload.

Start by sorting it

There are three different money systems, and they don't talk to each other.

Almost every funding mistake comes from asking the wrong system. Before you call anyone, work out which of these three you're actually dealing with — the answer decides who you call and what they owe you.

System one

The school district

Anything about your child's education. This is an obligation, not a benefit — the district must provide it, at no cost to you.

  • Evaluations
  • Everything written on the IEP
  • Speech, OT, PT delivered at school
  • Special class or program placement
  • Transportation when the IEP requires it

System two

Health insurance

Medical and therapeutic treatment outside of school. Private plan, Medicaid, or both. New York law requires many plans to cover autism treatment.

  • Diagnosis and evaluation
  • ABA, speech, OT, PT in a clinic or at home
  • Doctor visits
  • Some equipment and devices

OPWDD and the waiver

Long-term supports for developmental disability, funded through the state. This is the one families most often don't know exists.

  • Respite
  • Community habilitation
  • Assistive technology

Before you spend anything

Start with what already costs you nothing.

Families pay out of pocket for services their child is already entitled to, usually because nobody told them otherwise. Work through this list before you open your wallet.

Free to you

The district's obligation, not a favor it's doing you

  • The evaluation. Ask in writing. Once you sign consent, the district has 60 calendar days to complete it. Write down the date you signed.
  • Early Intervention for children under 3, run by the Health Department. Free. You can refer your own child — no diagnosis or doctor's note needed first.
  • Everything written on the IEP. If speech twice a week is on there, it is owed. Not offered — owed.
  • An interpreter and a translated IEP, in your language, on request.
  • An RSA when the district can't staff a mandated service — a voucher to find your own provider at DOE expense.
  • Free advocacy. Advocates for Children and Disability Rights New York cost nothing and do this every day.

What's the difference between "the school does it" and "I pay for it"?

Short answer

If it's on the IEP, the district pays. If you want more of it, or want it somewhere else, that's the other two systems.

A child can have speech at school on the IEP and additional private speech paid by insurance. Those are two different services from two different systems, and neither cancels the other. Schools sometimes imply that private therapy means the district can reduce its own — that's not how the obligation works.

The reverse trips people up too: insurance won't usually cover something purely educational, because that's the district's job. Knowing which bucket a service sits in is most of the battle.

System two

Insurance, and the one question that decides everything.

Does my insurance have to cover autism treatment?

Short answer

Many New York plans must — but whether yours does depends on one thing most parents have never been asked.

New York's autism insurance law requires state-regulated health plans to cover the screening, diagnosis, and treatment of autism spectrum disorder. Treatment explicitly includes applied behavior analysis, along with therapeutic care from licensed providers such as speech, occupational, and physical therapists, and assistive communication devices.

Here's the catch

The mandate applies to fully insured, New York State–regulated plans. It generally does not apply to self-funded employer plans governed by federal ERISA law — where your employer pays the claims itself and an insurance company only administers them.

The card in your wallet looks identical either way. Large employers are the most likely to be self-funded, which means a parent with a good job at a big company can have weaker autism coverage than a neighbor on a marketplace plan.

Call HR or your plan and ask: "Is our health plan fully insured or self-funded?"

That one question tells you whether New York's mandate protects you. If the answer is self-funded, you're not without options — federal mental health parity rules may still apply — but you need to know which ground you're standing on.

Do this week
  1. Ask HR whether your plan is fully insured or self-funded. Write the answer down.
  2. Ask your plan directly: is prior authorization required for ABA, speech, OT, or PT?
  3. Ask whether the provider you want is in network. Out-of-network is where the surprise bills come from.
  4. If a claim is denied, don't stop there — New York has an external appeal process, and free help exists to use it.

System three

OPWDD — the door most families never find.

The Office for People With Developmental Disabilities funds long-term supports across a lifetime: respite, community habilitation, assistive technology, home modifications, and help into adulthood. Autism can qualify. Getting in has a specific order, and doing it out of order costs months.

  1. Gather your documents. A psychological evaluation is central — see the note below about what it must contain.
  2. Contact the Front Door at your regional office. This is the mandatory entry point for all OPWDD services.
  3. Attend a Front Door information session.
  4. Eligibility determination. You'll receive a letter.
  5. Assessment to identify your child's strengths, needs, and interests.
  6. Choose a Care Coordination Organization. They become your ongoing point of contact.
  7. A Life Plan is written and services begin.
The thing families get wrong

"We make too much money to qualify." Usually, that's not how this works.

For a child living at home seeking enrollment in the HCBS Waiver, parental income and resources are not counted. The waiver looks at the child, not the household.

Families rule themselves out of years of support based on an assumption nobody ever corrected. If you've never applied because you assumed your salary disqualified your child, that assumption is worth checking.

Careful with this one: the rule is specific to children living at home seeking HCBS Waiver enrollment. Other OPWDD services have different cost-of-care rules. Confirm your own situation with the Front Door — don't take a website's word for it, including this one.

Ask for this before the evaluation, not after

Make sure the psychological evaluation includes adaptive behavior testing.

Eligibility generally rests on documentation of substantial functional limitations, and an evaluation without adaptive behavior testing often can't establish that. If it's missing, the process stalls while you go back and get it.

Tell the evaluator up front that the report may be used for an OPWDD eligibility determination. It costs nothing to ask and can save months.

Two different decisions

Being found eligible for OPWDD is not the same as being enrolled in the waiver.

Families are sometimes approved for eligibility and then not enrolled in the waiver right away, particularly for younger children, with an expectation that other supports get tried first. If that happens to you, it isn't the end of the process — but you'll want someone who knows the system helping you with what comes next.

What costs families the most

Five mistakes, all of them expensive, all of them avoidable.

Paying privately for what's on the IEP

If a service is written into the IEP and isn't being delivered, ask about an RSA before you start paying for it yourself.

Assuming income disqualifies your child

For a child at home seeking HCBS Waiver enrollment, parental income isn't counted. This assumption costs families years.

Not knowing your plan type

Fully insured or self-funded changes whether New York's autism mandate applies to you at all. Most parents have never been told to ask.

An evaluation that's missing adaptive testing

The report gets used for more than one purpose. Ask before it's written, not after it's filed.

Accepting a denial as final

Insurance denials can be appealed, including externally. Free legal help exists specifically for this.

Requesting things verbally

Evaluations, interpreters, records — put every request in writing and keep a dated copy. Verbal requests leave you with nothing to point at.
