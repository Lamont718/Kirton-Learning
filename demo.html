01

What arrives

A parent sends the IEP. This is the language it comes in — accurate, careful, and written for a compliance file rather than for a nine-year-old or her mother.

Student: Maya R. (invented)
Grade: 4
Classification: Autism
Annual review: March
Annual Goal 1 — Reading, decoding

Given a list of unfamiliar two-syllable words containing closed and vowel-team syllables, Maya will decode 8 of 10 words accurately across 3 consecutive sessions.

Present level (March): Decodes single-syllable words reliably. Reads 3 of 10 unfamiliar two-syllable words accurately.

Annual Goal 2 — Reading, comprehension

Given a grade-level informational text, Maya will identify the main idea and two supporting details with 80% accuracy in 4 of 5 trials.

Present level (March): Identifies the main idea in 1 of 5 grade-level informational passages. Cites supporting details at approximately 40% accuracy.

Annual Goal 3 — Writing

Maya will compose a paragraph containing a topic sentence and at least two supporting reasons, with 3 or fewer adult prompts, in 4 of 5 opportunities.

Present level (March): Produces a topic sentence in 2 of 5 opportunities. Requires an average of 7 adult prompts to complete a paragraph.

Annual Goal 4 — Mathematics

Maya will solve two-step word problems using addition, subtraction, and multiplication within 1,000, showing her work, with 75% accuracy across 4 of 5 trials.

Present level (March): Solves single-step problems within 100 at 80% accuracy. Solves 1 of 5 two-step problems with work shown.

Management needs: Advance notice of transitions. Reduced auditory input during independent work. Extended time. Responds well to predictable routine.

Nothing here is wrong. But read it again and notice: Maya appears four times, always as the object of the sentence. There is not one word about what she likes. That is the gap we work in.

02

The Blueprint

Delivered five business days after the IEP arrives. Every goal in plain English, a year mapped across four expeditions, and the first one built around what this specific child is into. Maya draws sneakers. Constantly.

Goal 1 — decode 8 of 10 unfamiliar two-syllable words, closed and vowel-team syllables, 3 consecutive sessions.

Break longer words into chunks and read them without guessing — starting with the words printed on the shoes she already owns.

Goal 2 — main idea and two supporting details, 80% accuracy, 4 of 5 trials.

Read something and say what it's actually about, then back it up with two specific things it said.

Goal 3 — paragraph with topic sentence and two supporting reasons, 3 or fewer prompts.

Make an argument in writing. State your case up front, then give two reasons someone should agree with you.

Goal 4 — two-step word problems within 1,000, showing work, 75%.

Work out a real cost that takes two steps to figure — and show the steps clearly enough that someone else can check them.

First expedition · weeks 1–9

Design a sneaker. Pitch it to somebody.

Maya designs a shoe — the materials, the colorway, the name — prices out what it would cost to make, and writes the pitch for why it should exist. At week 9 she presents it to her mother and her cousin.

  • Goal 1 lives in the materials list. Midsole, outsole, eyelet, canvas, cushion, stitching, padding, upper. Every one is a two-syllable word she has a reason to read.
  • Goal 2 lives in the research. She reads sneaker reviews and pulls out what the reviewer actually thinks, plus two things they said to prove it.
  • Goal 3 lives in the pitch. Topic sentence: why this shoe should exist. Two reasons. Written to a real person who will read it.
  • Goal 4 lives in the costing. Materials plus labor. Two steps. Work shown, because someone is going to check the number.

How we'll know it's working

A plan that can't be checked is a wish. The IEP records where the school measured Maya in March. We start in September, with a different person in a different room, so the first thing built is a fresh baseline — and a written rule for what counts as evidence and what doesn't.

Week 0 — the baseline, before any teaching

Target by week 36

8 of 10, three sessions running

Goal 2 · comprehension

33% — 1 of 3 questions right across two passages

Target by week 36

80%, in 4 of 5 trials

Goal 3 · writing
School, March

Topic sentence and two reasons, 3 prompts or fewer, 4 of 5

Target by week 36

That last line is the useful one. Maya can already do more of the arithmetic than her file suggests — what she isn't doing is showing it. That changes what gets taught, and it would have stayed invisible without a baseline.

What counts as data, and what doesn't

  • Practice is not measurement. Monday through Thursday is coached work. She gets help, she gets second tries, and none of it goes in as a score.
  • A probe is cold. Material she has never seen, no help while it's happening, no correction until it's finished. That's the only thing recorded as a number.
  • Never probe with the week's own material. If a score jumps, the first thing to check is whether this week's words leaked into the probe — not whether she had a breakthrough.
  • Self-corrections count. If she catches it herself within a few seconds and no adult said anything, it's correct. That's what reading actually is.

How often, and how long it takes

Goal 1 · decoding

Every week · 10 unfamiliar words · about 4 minutes

Goal 2 · comprehension

Even weeks · one unseen passage, 3 questions · about 15 minutes

Goal 4 · mathematics

Even weeks · 4 new two-step problems · about 15 minutes

Goal 3 · writing

Odd weeks · one paragraph written alone, prompts counted as they happen · about 20 minutes

Writing sits on the opposite week from the other two on purpose. An even week costs about 35 minutes of measurement spread across four days; an odd week costs about 24. It has to be small enough that it actually gets done.

What happens when it isn't working

  • Four probes in a row below the line and we change the teaching, not the goal. The criterion in the IEP does not move because the work got hard.
  • You hear it from me first. A flat month gets said out loud on the monthly call before you read it in the Log. Nobody should discover a stall by scrolling.
  • Some goals stall for reasons the plan can't fix. If that's what the data says, that's what I'll tell you — and it may be worth raising with her team.

What this measurement is not. It is collected at a kitchen table by a parent and by me — not in a controlled setting, not by an evaluator, and not under the conditions a school uses. It is not progress reporting and it does not replace the school's. The softest number here is the prompt count, because the adult counting the prompts is the same adult who wants her to do well; the rule is to write each one down as it happens, not from memory afterwards. And if our numbers and the school's disagree, that is information — different room, different person, different day — not an argument.

Same four goals in every expedition. The standards never soften. What changes is that the child has a reason to hit them.

Week 6, as it actually lands in a parent's inbox on Sunday night. Three to four sessions, 20–40 minutes each, built for a school night after a long day.

Monday

Read the shoe

Twelve material words, one at a time. Untimed, and nothing marks her wrong — a word she misses comes back in chunks so she can go again.

~20 min

~25 min

What does the sole cost?

Write the pitch

One paragraph. Why should this shoe exist? Say it in the first line, then give two reasons. Her cousin is the reader.

The Log

This is the part that changes the next meeting. Everything Maya does gets written down against the goal it belongs to — reported back in the goal's own words, so the numbers line up with the language already in her file.

Two kinds of entry, and the difference matters. Probe rows are the cold, uncoached checks — those are the numbers. Practice rows are the week's actual work, which is coached and is never scored. Week 6 is an even week, so decoding, comprehension and mathematics were all probed.

"The Rainmaker should get made because kids need a shoe that works when it's wet outside. The bottom has grip so you don't slip on the wet part. And it's under fifty dollars so a kid can actually buy it."

Take that page into the annual review. It is dated, it is written in the same language as the goals themselves, and it is the child's own work. You will not be the only person in the room without paper.
