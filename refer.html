For people who work with these families

You already know the parent this is for.

She has sat in an annual review as the only person at the table without documentation. This is a supplemental service that gives her a year of her child's work, written down against the goals already in the file. Here is exactly what it is, what it isn't, and who I'd want you to send — and who I wouldn't.

What it is

A parent sends me their child's IEP. I read the annual goals and build a 36-week plan that carries those same goals inside work the child actually chose — a shoe they want to design, a thing wrong on their block, something they want to sell. The criteria don't move. A goal that says 8 of 10 words across 3 consecutive sessions still says that. What changes is that the child has a reason to hit it.

Every week gets logged, dated, and written back in the goal's own language, so the parent walks into the next meeting with a record instead of an impression.

What it is

  • Supplemental instruction a parent hires privately
  • Built from the goals already written in the IEP
  • A dated log of work completed, in the goal's language
  • Designed around one child's actual interests
  • Delivered weekly, with a monthly call

What it is not

  • Not advocacy, and not legal advice
  • Not an evaluation, a diagnosis, or an eligibility determination
  • Not a related service and not a mandated service — it counts toward no minutes on the IEP
  • Not a replacement for anything the district provides
  • Not a claim to meet, satisfy, or fulfill any IEP requirement

On the school. This service takes no position on a district, a placement, or a team. If a parent wants to bring the Log to a meeting, it's hers and she should. But I'm not there to build a case against anybody — she has to keep living in that building, and so does her child.

Don't take my word for it

There's a complete worked example online — an invented student, her four annual goals, the plan built from them, all thirty-six weeks, one week in full detail, and the log entries. It's the fastest way to judge whether this is serious. It takes about six minutes to read.

Who I'd want you to send

Right fit

A parent who wants her child doing something, not doing more worksheets. A child with a strong interest — the narrower and more intense, the better. Roughly grades 2 through 8. A family that can commit to three or four short sessions a week at home.

Also right

A parent heading toward an annual review with nothing written down, who wants a record of the year in her own hands.

Wrong fit

A family in an active dispute who needs an advocate or an attorney — that isn't me and I'd be doing them harm by taking the money. A child whose needs are primarily medical, behavioral crisis, or safety-related. A parent looking for a diagnosis or an evaluation. Anyone who needs this to substitute for services the district is supposed to provide.

Be honest with me

If you think a family is a bad fit, say so and I won't take them. I'd rather have five families this works for than twenty it doesn't.

Where this honestly stands

You work in this field and you'll find this out anyway, so I'd rather say it first.

This is new. It started with my own niece, who is autistic, and it worked for her. That is one child, and one child is a reason to try something — not evidence. I have no outcome data and I won't pretend to. I'm looking for the first ten families.

Checkout isn't switched on yet. If you have a family in mind now, send them straight to me and I'll take them through by hand.

What I'd want from you most is the part I'm weakest on. I'm not a certified special educator. I've run youth programs in Brooklyn since 2009 — adaptive basketball with DYCD, chess and computer science at P.S. 109 and P.S. 272 — and I build this work with real kids. But you know the measurement side better than I do, and if you ever want to tell me a baseline is missing or a goal isn't being tracked honestly, that's a favor, not a criticism.

What I'm asking

  1. Read the example.

    Six minutes. If it doesn't hold up to you, tell me why and we're done — that's still useful to me.

  2. If it does hold up, keep one family in mind.

    Not a list. One parent you think it would genuinely help. Send her the example page, or send me her name with her permission and I'll reach out.

  3. Tell me what you'd change.

    You've read more IEPs than I have. If something in the plan or the log would embarrass me at a CSE table, I want to hear it now rather than later.

On paying you for referrals. If you work for a school, a district, or an agency — or if any part of your work is billed to Medicaid — I'm not offering you a referral fee, and you shouldn't accept one. It's a conflict of interest that lands on your license and your job, not on mine. What I'd offer instead: a free spot for a family of yours who can't pay, the Blueprint process documented for you to use with your own caseload, and your name on this work if you ever want it there.

If you're fully independent and none of that applies, we can talk about a straightforward arrangement — and either way, every family gets told plainly that you're the one who sent them.
